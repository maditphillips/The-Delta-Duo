The Coordinator Bump

The claim: hire an offensive coordinator as your head coach and you win three more games than last year. Across 179 hires, the number is 1.7 — and almost all of it is the bounce any bad team gets, no matter who it hires.

179 hires · 26 seasons 648-season control group Source: nflverse games.csv

Verdict

Not supported as stated. Offensive coordinators promoted to head coach gained an average of +1.7 wins in year one (95% CI +0.7 to +2.6). Three wins sits outside that interval.

The bump is also not an OC thing. Every hiring lane produced roughly the same year-one gain, because the gain is mostly regression to the mean: teams that fire a coach averaged 5.5 wins, and 5.5-win teams improve by about two wins whether or not they change anything.

But there is a real version of the claim. Since 2017, OC hires have gained +3.3 wins in year one and held it through year three, while every other hiring lane went flat. That split is the actual finding here.

What the data is

Every opening-week coaching change, 2000–2025

nflverse's schedule file carries the head coach for both teams on every game back to 1999, alongside the score. That yields 861 team-seasons and 181 changes of opening-day head coach. Two are dropped — New Orleans in 2012 and 2013, where Sean Payton's season-long suspension put a caretaker in the chair — leaving 179 hires.

What nflverse does not carry is the job each coach held immediately before. Those 179 prior roles were labeled by hand into five lanes, and the label file ships with the analysis so any call can be checked or overturned.

The raw answer

Wins gained versus the season before the hire

Taken at face value, every lane looks like a success. That is the first warning sign.

The problem with that chart is the group it is drawn from. A team that fires its head coach is, by construction, a bad team — the 179 hiring teams averaged 5.5 wins the year before. And bad NFL teams get better on their own.

Fitting the 648 team-seasons in this window where the coach did not change gives the size of that pull:

Mean-reversion model · n = 648 no-change team-seasons

win% next = 0.336 + 0.334 × win% prior

Two-thirds of a team's distance from .500 evaporates in a single season with no intervention at all. For a 5.5-win team that is a predicted +1.9 wins — before the new coach has called a single play.

Subtracting the bounce

What the coach actually adds

Scoring each hire against what a team of that exact quality was expected to do anyway leaves the residual — the part attributable to the change itself. The chart collapses.

This is the central result. Averaged over 26 years, the lane a head coach comes from does not predict how much his team improves. The apparent +1.5 to +2.0 win bump in the raw chart is the regression the team was owed regardless.

Two secondary patterns survive the correction, and both cut against the original claim.

OC hires front-load; DC hires compound

Offensive coordinators post their best year first (+1.7 wins) and fade to +1.3 by year three. Defensive coordinators start slowest (+1.2) and are still climbing at +2.4. In net points per game — a less noisy measure than wins — the year-three gap is +4.9 for DC hires against +1.5 for OC hires, the only OC-versus-DC difference in the whole study that reaches significance (p = 0.032), and it points the opposite direction from the claim.

Coaches fix the side of the ball they came from

OC hires improve scoring and leave the defense alone. DC hires improve the defense (−2.2 points allowed per game by year two, the largest defensive effect of any lane) while their offense goes backwards. The specialization is real; it just doesn't add up to more wins for one side than the other.

Where the claim is true

Splitting by era

Pooling 26 years hides the one place the number holds up. Split the hires into three eras and the OC lane changes character completely.

The modern split is not a rounding artifact. Against every other 2017–2025 hire, the OC group's residual is +2.4 wins better (p = 0.003); against pre-2017 OC hires it is +1.7 better (p = 0.040). Dropping the single best and single worst case leaves the mean at +3.3. And it holds: +3.3 in year one, +3.1 in year two, +3.0 in year three.

Fourteen of these 23 hires cleared +3 wins outright.

Trends worth keeping

Six findings

  • The bump is real but small, and it is not about the coach. Median hire gains +1.9 wins; 38% gain nothing or get worse. Only 35% reach the +3 the claim treats as typical.
  • Mean reversion is the dominant force. Two-thirds of a team's deviation from .500 disappears in one year without any intervention. Any coaching study that skips this control will find effects that are not there.
  • Lane choice historically did not matter — timing of the payoff did. OC hires peak in year one; DC hires peak in years two and three and end up ahead on points.
  • The modern OC premium is the genuine signal. Since 2017 it is large, statistically distinguishable, and stable across three seasons — the McVay–Shanahan–LaFleur generation, plus Daboll, O'Connell, Steichen, Ben Johnson and Liam Coen.
  • Defensive coordinators keep their jobs. 85% are still the opening head coach in year three, against 67% of OC hires and 47% of college hires — despite similar win records. Slower, steadier improvement appears to buy patience.
  • College hires are the highest-variance lane. Best raw gains (+2.6 wins), worst survival, and a residual of roughly zero. They are hired by the worst teams, which flatters the raw numbers.

How to read this

Method and limits

Sample. All opening-day head-coach changes, 2000–2025, from nflverse games.csv. Regular season only. Franchise moves (STL/LA, SD/LAC, OAK/LV) are treated as continuous. Years 2 and 3 measure the team, not the coach, against the pre-hire baseline — so a fired coach's replacement still counts, which is the right way to score the decision to hire him.

The 17-game problem. Seasons went from 16 to 17 games in 2021, which inflates raw win differences for any hire spanning that line. Every "wins" figure above is win-percentage change scaled to 17 games. Raw counts are in the shipped tables and differ by less than 0.1 wins overall.

The labels. Prior roles were assigned by hand from knowledge, not scraped — Wikipedia and Pro-Football-Reference are unreachable from this environment. Fifteen hires are flagged ambiguous, mostly assistant-head-coach and interim titles that could sit in two lanes (Sean Payton in 2006, Jim Caldwell in 2009, Dan Campbell in 2021). Dropping all fifteen moves no bucket mean by more than 0.1 wins except "Other," which is small either way.

The era split is post-hoc. The 2017 boundary was chosen after seeing the data, so its p-values are optimistic — the honest reading is that the modern OC premium is a strong hypothesis with a plausible mechanism (offense-driven rule changes, a generation of play-callers on rookie-contract quarterbacks), not a settled result. It needs the next few hiring cycles to confirm.

Not controlled for. Quarterback change, roster turnover, draft position, schedule strength, and front-office change. A team that hires a new coach and drafts a franchise quarterback in the same offseason credits both to the coach here.
