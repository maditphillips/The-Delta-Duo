How Predictive Is Week 1?

One game, 32 teams, and a whole week of takes. We ran every Week 1 against the rest of its own season — usage, targets, yards per carry, snap counts, completion percentage, fantasy points — to find which numbers were telling the truth.

55 position-metric pairs · regular seasons only · snap share 2013+

The short version

Week 1 tells you about role. It tells you almost nothing about skill.

Split every metric into opportunity (how much work a player got, and what share of his team's it was) and efficiency (what he did with it). The two behave nothing alike after one game. Everything below is a version of that one sentence.

opportunity metrics0.50 average correlation between a Week 1 opportunity number and the rest of the season
efficiency metrics0.16 the same average for per-play efficiency — roughly a third as much signal
Week 1 yards per carry6% share of one game's spread in RB yards per carry that is real. It needs 17 games to get to half signal
Week 1 share of carries73% the same figure for an RB's share of his team's carries. Half a game gets you there

Same box score, same Sunday. One column is worth reading and the other is a coin flip with a decimal point.

The control

Week 1 is not special. It is just one game.

Every claim above rests on Week 1 being read as one game and no more. So we ran the identical test on a mid-season week — each of weeks 2 through 9 against the rest of its own season. New schemes, new rosters, no film: Week 1 should be the noisiest week of the year, or the most revealing. It is neither.

The playbook

What to actually do on Tuesday morning

  1. Open the snap counts before the box score. Snap share is the one Week 1 number that outpredicts a full prior season at every position, and a running back's share of team carries does it too. Almost everything else needs two to four more weeks to catch up.
  2. Read targets, not receptions or yards. Target share is the best single Week 1 predictor of rest-of-season fantasy points at wide receiver and tight end. Catch rate and yards per target are noise.
  3. Throw out Week 1 yards per carry entirely. It needs about a season and a half of carries to become half signal. A 2.4-yard opener and a 6.1-yard opener tell you the same thing: nothing.
  4. Do not move a quarterback on completion percentage. One game of it is roughly one-sixth signal, and last season predicts this season's rate about twice as well as Sunday did.
  5. Buy role changes, not performances. A back who took 70% of the snaps out of nowhere keeps most of that job. A back who broke one long run keeps nothing.
  6. Discount touchdowns to near zero. Receiving and rushing touchdowns are the least stable counting stats measured here. Week 1 scoring is mostly next week's regression.
  7. Do not sell a proven player off one bad Sunday. The panic table is the price sheet: the buyer is paying for a name that still finishes top-12 about half the time.

Every one of these is the same rule wearing a different jersey: after one game you know who is on the field, and you do not yet know how good they are.

Method & caveats

How this was built

Data
nflverse weekly player stats, team stats and snap counts, plus nfldata game results and closing lines. Regular seasons 1999–2025; snap counts only exist from 2013, so every snap figure uses that shorter window. Half-PPR is derived as PPR minus half a point per catch.
Unit
The player-season, with three views of each metric: Week 1, weeks 2 onward, and the player's previous full regular season. Volume is per game; rates are pooled sum-over-sum with a minimum rest-of-season denominator, so a three-carry sample cannot pose as a yards-per-carry.
Signal share
A one-way random-effects split of game-to-game variance. With λ = noise ÷ real spread, a k-game sample has reliability k/(k+λ). This holds a player's true level fixed inside a season, so a genuine mid-season role change counts as noise — which makes the games-to-stabilise column an upper bound, not a floor.
Survivorship
The default sample needs four games after Week 1, which answers “if he stays on the field, what is he?” and strips out injury risk. The unconditional version, where missed weeks count as zero, runs alongside it in the repo and lowers every volume correlation by roughly 0.03 to 0.11.
What we cut
EPA per play does not beat the Week 1 scoreboard at predicting the rest of the season, at any sample size tested here. It was a cleaner story and the numbers did not support it, so it is not on this page.
Reproducing it
scripts/analysis/week1-predictiveness — fetch.py then analyze.py. Every figure on this page is generated from the study's own output files; none are typed by hand.
